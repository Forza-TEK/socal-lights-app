# Contest organizer pricing: competitor comparison (2026-11-07)

> **UPDATE 2026-11-09 (owner decision, after meeting the City of Eastvale who recommended charging): organizers pay from the first contest.** The plan analyzed below (1 free contest per 12 months; $50 / $75 / $100) is **superseded**. New prices: **single contest $149, up to 5 contests in a 12-month window $299, up to 20 contests $499**, more than 20 contact us; residents still enter and vote free and payment never affects results. Against the comparables in this file: HOA voting software TownSq charges about **$250 per voting event** (a single contest at $149 is below it; 5 contests at $299 is about $60 each, against $1,250 at TownSq's rate), and Woobox's cheapest paid plan is **$29 per month** (about $348 a year, with entry and vote caps; our $299 covers 5 contests with no caps). The new prices stay under the strictest small-purchase card limit found below (Turlock, $500 per transaction): $499 is the ceiling that analysis named. The tables below keep the old figures for the record; read "Ours: $0 / $50 / $100" as "Ours now: $149 / $299 / $499".


Purpose: sanity-check our planned organizer prices (1 free contest per 12 months; passes for total contests in the year: up to 5 = $50, up to 10 = $75, up to 20 = $100, over 20 = contact us; residents enter and vote free; payment never affects results). All "accessed" dates are 2026-11-07. "Verified" means the figure was read on the vendor's own page. "Low confidence" means a third-party listing or search snippet only. Nothing here is guessed; gaps are marked "not public" or "not verified". Prices are USD unless noted.

## Summary

Our prices are far below every comparable we could verify, and the comparison is not close. The cheapest verified paid plan for a single contest is $29 for one month (Woobox Basic, ShortStack Launch, SweepWidget Pro), and the cheapest verified annual plan is $290/yr. Our $50 pass covers up to 5 contests; the same coverage costs $290 to $2,880 a year on contest SaaS and about $2,400 to $6,500 a year on judged-awards software. The only closer comparable is HOA software: TownSq sells a "Digital Voting" add-on at $250 per voting event plus $25 setup, on top of a $90+/month community plan.

Conclusions:
1. **Under the market, by 5x to 100x.** Our $50/$75/$100 vs. $290/yr (cheapest annual self-serve), $1,999/yr (Easypromos Premium, the tier that carries photo contests), $2,399/yr (Judgify Pro Unlimited), $3,250/yr (Award Force Growth).
2. **We are cheapest where it matters most:** a city running 1 to 5 contests a year pays $0 to $50. Free tiers elsewhere are capped hard (Woobox Free: 100 participants/submits per month, and votes count as participants; ShortStack Free: 200 stored entries, 500 views/month, and no public voting contest on Free).
3. **We are relatively most expensive at the free-to-$50 step, not at the top.** A second contest in a year moves a user from $0 to $50. That is still less than one month of any self-serve rival ($29 is the floor, and public voting needs the next plan up on ShortStack), so it is defensible. The 20-contest user at $100 is where we are most underpriced relative to the alternatives ($290 to $2,880/yr).
4. **Every tier clears the strictest California small-purchase limit we found.** Turlock's card limit is $500 per transaction; Los Alamitos allows card use under $2,500; Fort Bragg and Sacramento use $5,000. Staff can pay $50 to $100 by card without quotes or council action. This is a real selling point and caps how high we can go without friction (about $499).
5. **Positioning is "hosting capacity for a local civic contest", not "marketing software".** Rivals sell lead capture, giveaways and fraud suites for businesses. We sell a finished, local, no-code contest with a free public audience. Prices should anchor against the $250/voting-event HOA benchmark and against staff time, not against $29/month giveaway tools.

## Purchasing thresholds for California cities

Each city sets its own limits; there is no statewide figure.

| City | Rule (dollar figure) | Source (accessed 2026-11-07) |
|---|---|---|
| Turlock | Card: $500 per transaction, $2,000 per billing cycle; quote or bid needed over $500 | https://www.cityofturlock.org/_pdf/hrwebpp.asp?id=90 |
| Los Alamitos | Card (Cal-Card) under $2,500; purchase order required above $2,500; "open market" up to $10,000 on department director approval; informal bids $10,000 to $100,000 | https://cityoflosalamitos.org/DocumentCenter/View/5354/502---Purchasing-Authority-Thresholds-PDF |
| Fort Bragg | $5,000 or less in 12 months: quotes optional, no PO; over $5,000 to $60,000: three quotes and a PO; card purchases limited to $5,000; City Manager signs up to $60,000 | https://www.codepublishing.com/CA/FortBragg/html/FortBragg03/FortBragg0320.html |
| Sacramento | Purchase card limit normally $5,000, the discretionary amount below which no competitive bidding is required | https://www.cityofsacramento.gov/content/dam/portal/city-government/policies-procedures/PurchaseCardProgram.pdf |

Seen only in search snippets (pages blocked, lower confidence): Monrovia no bids under $5,000; Pittsburg quotes at $10,000 and under; Calexico City Manager authority raised from $10,000 to $25,000; Livermore small purchases under $10,000.
Implications: $50, $75 and $100 sit below every card or quote threshold found, including the strictest ($500). A $499 ceiling would still clear all four. Above $500, expect quotes in some cities, and above $2,500 to $5,000 a purchase order.

W-9, invoice, PO: a W-9 on file before payment is the norm (search snippets citing Fullerton and San Francisco vendor rules; the pages themselves returned 403, so low confidence). A purchase order is commonly required before work for larger amounts, but small card purchases usually skip it. For $50 to $100 paid by card, no W-9 or PO should be needed; we should still be ready to send a PDF invoice and a W-9 on request.

## Recommendations

1. **Keep $0 / $50 and the one-free-per-12-months rule.** It is far below the market and below every purchasing threshold. The free contest is the main acquisition lever for cities.
2. **Consider raising the top tier, not the bottom.** A 20-contest customer is a chamber, agency or management company getting a lot of value for $100. Options: keep up to 20 = $100 for launch, then test $150 to $200 (still under the $500 Turlock card limit). The $75 middle tier is thin; fold it into $50 (up to 10) or lift it to $99.
3. **Define "organization" carefully.** An HOA management company serving many communities should not share one $100 pass. TownSq prices per community, which supports per-organization or per-community passes.
4. **Position against staff time and the $250 HOA voting benchmark**, not against $29/month giveaway tools: "less than one month of other contest software for a full year of contests".
5. **Naming:** "Pass" is fine for the paid step. Consider "Season Pass" language tied to the holiday calendar, and call the free contest "First contest free" rather than a tier. Always state "residents vote free; payment never affects results" next to the price, since paid-vote competitors (GoGo) are the legal contrast.
6. **Keep payment frictionless:** card checkout, PDF receipt, W-9 on request. Avoid prices above $499 for self-serve.
7. **Recheck Gleam, Submittable and OpenWater by hand** (browser, or a sales inquiry) before quoting any competitor price in public materials.
