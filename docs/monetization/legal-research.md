## 0. Correction to earlier docs

`docs/contest-business-model.md` cites B&P 17534 as the California contest law. It is not. Section 17534 is only the penalty clause for the whole chapter (B&P Div. 7, Pt. 3, Ch. 1, which starts at 17500, plus the 17530s):

> Any person, firm, corporation, partnership or association or any employee or agent thereof who violates this chapter is guilty of a misdemeanor.
> (B&P 17534; updated Jan. 1, 1941; accessed Nov. 2, 2026)

The real contest provisions are B&P 17539 through 17539.35, plus Penal Code 319 to 322 (the lottery). Section 17536 adds a civil penalty "not to exceed two thousand five hundred dollars ($2,500) for each violation" (AG, DA, county counsel or city attorney can sue).

`docs/rules-review.md` leans on B&P 17539.15. That section is about "sweepstakes" solicitation materials, and its own text is built around mailings and "entry-order devices". It is a useful style guide but does not obviously govern a free skill-and-vote contest (see 1.4).

## 1. California contest and lottery law

Source for all of 1: https://california.public.law/codes/ca_penal_code_section_319 and the B&P URLs in Sources.

### 1.1 The lottery (Penal Code)

> A lottery is any scheme for the disposal or distribution of property by chance, among persons who have paid or promised to pay any valuable consideration for the chance of obtaining such property or a portion of it, or for any share or any interest in such property, upon any agreement, understanding, or expectation that it is to be distributed or disposed of by lot or chance, whether called a lottery, raffle, or gift enterprise, or by whatever name the same may be known.
> (Penal 319; accessed Nov. 2, 2026)

> Every person who contrives, prepares, sets up, proposes, or draws any lottery, is guilty of a misdemeanor.
> (Penal 320)

> Every person who aids or assists, either by printing, writing, advertising, publishing, or otherwise in setting up, managing, or drawing any lottery ... is guilty of a misdemeanor.
> (Penal 322, excerpt)

**Statute says:** the three elements are (1) property (a prize), (2) distribution "by chance", (3) among persons who "paid or promised to pay any valuable consideration". Section 322 reaches anyone who "aids or assists ... by ... advertising, publishing", which could include the website host.

### 1.2 Contest and sweepstakes provisions (B&P 17539 to 17539.35)

> The Legislature finds that there is a compelling need for more complete disclosure of rules and operation of contests in which money or other valuable consideration may be solicited ...
> (B&P 17539, excerpt)

The key definition (this is what makes the disclosure rules apply):

> As used in Section 17539 to 17539.2, inclusive, "contest" includes any game, contest, puzzle, scheme, or plan that holds out or offers to prospective participants the opportunity to receive or compete for gifts, prizes, or gratuities as determined by skill or any combination of chance and skill and that is, or in whole or in part may be, conditioned upon the payment of consideration.
> (B&P 17539.3(e); updated Jan. 1, 2007; accessed Nov. 2, 2026)

Obligations of a person who conducts such a "contest":

> (a) Clearly and conspicuously disclose on each entry blank the deadline for submission of that entry. (b) Refund all money or other consideration to contestants requesting such refund in writing within one year of payment and who are unable to participate ... (c) At the conclusion of the contest send to all entrants upon their request the names of all winners, the prize or prizes won by each ... (d) Maintain for no less than two years after all prizes are awarded ... Copies of all contest solicitations ... records ... disclos[ing] the names and addresses of all contestants ... the number of prizes awarded, the method of selecting winners ...
> (B&P 17539.2, excerpts)

Prohibited acts in "any contest or sweepstakes" (17539.1(a)), the ones relevant to us:

> (3) Misrepresenting in any manner the odds of winning any prize. (4) Misrepresenting in any manner, the rules, terms, or conditions of participation in a contest. ... (6) Failing to clearly and conspicuously disclose the exact nature and approximate value of the prizes when offered. (7) Failing to award and distribute all prizes of the value and type represented. ... (11) Using the word "lucky" ...
> (B&P 17539.1(a), excerpts; updated Jan. 1, 2026; accessed Oct. 26, 2026)

17539.1(a)(1) and (5) add puzzle/contestant-count disclosures "each time the payment of money is required". Nothing in 17539.1 requires registration or a bond.

Other points:

> No person shall advertise, offer, or operate any contest, as defined in subdivision (e) of Section 17539.3, in which any prize ... may be awarded or transferred if the opportunity to win that prize is conditioned on a minimum number of entries or contest participants.
> (B&P 17539.35)

> "person" includes a firm, corporation, or association, but does not include any charitable trust, corporation, or other organization exempted from taxation under Section 23701d of the Revenue and Taxation Code or Section 501(c) of the Internal Revenue Code.
> (B&P 17539.3(b), excerpt)

> Nothing in Section 17539 to 17539.2, inclusive, shall be construed to permit any contest or any series of contests ... that is prohibited by any other provision of law.
> (B&P 17539.3(c), excerpt)

### 1.3 Sweepstakes (17539.15) and the "no purchase" line

> "Sweepstakes" means a procedure, activity, or event, for the distribution, donation, or sale of anything of value by lot, chance, predetermined selection, or random selection that is not unlawful under other provisions of law ...
> (B&P 17539.1(b)(3))

> Solicitation materials containing sweepstakes entry materials ... shall include a clear and conspicuous statement of the no-purchase-or-payment-necessary message ... The no-purchase-or-payment-necessary message ... shall be set out in a separate paragraph in the official rules and be printed in capital letters in contrasting typeface not smaller than the largest typeface used in the text of the official rules.
> (B&P 17539.15(b); updated Jan. 1, 2009; accessed Nov. 2, 2026)

> The official rules for a sweepstakes shall disclose information about the date or dates the final winner or winners will be determined.
> (B&P 17539.15(j))

Also relevant to the word "prize": B&P 17537(a) says it is unlawful to use "prize" or "gift" "in any manner that would be untrue or misleading", and 17537(b) bars telling someone they "won a prize" on condition they "pay any money or purchase or rent any goods or services". The AG's consumer page (oag.ca.gov/consumers/general/sweepstakes, read 2026-11-04) says: "Legitimate sweepstakes are free and by chance. It is illegal to require you to buy something or pay to enter or increase your odds of winning a sweepstakes." and "Raffles are only legal if they are conducted by a charitable organization registered with the California Attorney General's Office ... at least 90% of the raffle's gross receipts are used for charitable purposes."

### 1.4 What this means for our model (plain English)

| Feature | Statute says | My reading |
|---|---|---|
| **Free entry, free voting** | Lottery needs "valuable consideration" (Penal 319). The B&P "contest" definition applies only if participation "is, or in whole or in part may be, conditioned upon the payment of consideration" (17539.3(e)). | With no payment, no lottery element and no "contest" under 17539.3(e), so 17539.1(a)(1)-(5) puzzle disclosures, 17539.2 record-keeping and refunds should not apply. Whether signing up, giving an email, or watching a sponsor logo counts as "consideration" is the open question for the attorney. Cheap to follow anyway: state the deadline, prize value, method, keep records two years. |
| **Judges** | Lottery needs "chance". | Judging by a panel against criteria looks like skill. A popularity vote is not clearly skill or chance; I could not retrieve case law. Free entry makes this less important. |
| **Sponsor-funded prizes** | 17539.1(a)(6),(7): disclose "exact nature and approximate value", and "award and distribute all prizes of the value and type represented". 17539.15(l)(2)(B): a person who "merely furnishes a prize" is not a "sweepstakes sponsor". | Prizes from third parties are not the problem; misdescribing them is. Our rules say "Prizes have no cash value" and that the organizer, not SoCal Lights, is responsible. The attorney should confirm that disclaimer is accurate and that we are not the "person conducting" the contest. |
| **No entry fee** | Same as the free-entry row. | Keep entry free. A fee would likely bring in 17539.1, 17539.2 and 17539.35, and add lottery risk if any chance remains. |
| **Paid votes** | 17539.3(e) covers skill or chance contests "conditioned upon the payment of consideration". Penal 319 counts "paid ... any valuable consideration for the chance". | Paid votes are consideration that buys advantage in a prize contest. That pulls in the B&P contest rules (refunds, records, disclosures, no minimum-entries conditions) and, to the extent chance remains, an unlawful lottery under Penal 320-322. The AG page says it is illegal "to require you to buy something or pay to enter or increase your odds". I recommend never selling votes. |
| **Sponsor logos, "Presented by", paid placement** | No provision found that restricts advertising near a free contest. | Fine in principle if payment never affects entry or winner selection, and the sponsor cannot judge or vote. The risk is labelling (section 3). |
| **Registration/bonding** | None found in B&P 17539 to 17539.35 or on the AG page. | I found no California registration or bond requirement. Secondary sources (blog posts, e.g. Thompson Coburn, Woobox, search results 2026-11-04) say New York and Florida (over $5,000 in prizes) and Rhode Island (retail promotions of $500 or more) require registration, and NY/FL a bond, for *sweepstakes* in those states. I did not retrieve those statutes. Our rules limit entrants to homeowners in the contest area, and a free skill contest probably falls outside, but the attorney should confirm, particularly for voters outside California. |

Note on wording: `public/rules.js` calls the organizer the "Sponsor", and our product also sells "sponsors" (logo sponsors). The same word means two different roles, which could confuse a regulator or entrant.

## 2. Sponsor, prize and bonding rules

**I found none specific to California for a free contest.** Checked: B&P 17539, 17539.1, 17539.15, 17539.2, 17539.3, 17539.35 (all quoted above or summarized), and the AG's sweepstakes page. The only prize-linked duties are disclosure and delivery (17539.1(a)(6),(7)), the minimum-entries ban (17539.35), and, if consideration is ever required, record-keeping and refunds (17539.2). Out-of-state rules (NY, FL, RI) are secondary-source only, flagged above. Also check federal tax reporting for prizes of $600 or more (Form 1099; not researched here, a CPA question).

Nonprofit exemption: 17539.3(b) excludes 501(c) organizations from "person" for 17539.1-17539.2. A chamber of commerce or HOA that organizes a contest may therefore fall outside those two sections. My reading only.

## 3. FTC and disclosures

### 3.1 16 CFR Part 255 (Endorsement Guides)

Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/16/255.5 and /255.0 (accessed 2026-11-04). The eCFR versioner API lists the latest content version of Part 255 as dated 2023-07-26.

> For purposes of this part, "clear and conspicuous" means that a disclosure is difficult to miss (i.e., easily noticeable) and easily understandable by ordinary consumers. ... A visual disclosure, by its size, contrast, location, the length of time it appears, and other characteristics, should stand out from any accompanying text or other visual elements so that it is easily noticed, read, and understood.
> (16 CFR 255.0(f), excerpt)

> When there exists a connection between the endorser and the seller of the advertised product that might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement, and that connection is not reasonably expected by the audience, such connection must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously. Material connections can include a business, family, or personal relationship. They can include monetary payment or the provision of free or discounted products ... Material connections can also include other benefits to the endorser, such as early access to a product or the possibility of being paid, of winning a prize ...
> (16 CFR 255.5(a), excerpt)

An affiliate example appears in 255.5(b):

> A coffee lover creates a blog that reviews coffee makers. The blogger writes the content independently of the marketers of the coffee makers but includes affiliate links ... Whenever a consumer clicks on such a link and buys the product, the blogger receives a portion of the sale. Because knowledge of this compensation could affect the weight or credibility site visitors give to the blogger's reviews, the reviews should clearly and conspicuously disclose the compensation.

**My reading:** Part 255 applies to endorsements. Our "recommended" shop and ticket links with affiliate commission fit the blog example. A paid Featured placement is an advertisement, so the more relevant FTC text is the native advertising guidance below. The Guides are "administrative interpretations", not statute: 255.0(a) says violations may lead to "corrective action ... under section 5".

### 3.2 FTC Native Advertising Guide (December 2015)

Source: https://search.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/native-advertising-guide-businesses (the FTC's own search mirror; www.ftc.gov 404s). Primary (agency site), not a law-firm summary.

> A basic truth-in-advertising principle is that it's deceptive to mislead consumers about the commercial nature of content.
> If a disclosure is necessary to prevent deception, the disclosure must be clear and prominent.

> Terms likely to be understood include "Ad," "Advertisement," "Paid Advertisement," "Sponsored Advertising Content," or some variation thereof. Advertisers should not use terms such as "Promoted" or "Promoted Stories," which in this context are at best ambiguous ... Furthermore, depending on the context, consumers reasonably may interpret other terms, such as "Presented by [X]," "Brought to You by [X]," "Promoted by [X]," or "Sponsored by [X]" to mean that a sponsoring advertiser funded or "underwrote" but did not create or influence the content.

> In general, disclosures should be: in clear and unambiguous language; as close as possible to the native ads to which they relate; in a font and color that's easy to read; in a shade that stands out against the background ...
> Moreover, company logos and names on their own are not likely adequate to signal that content is commercial advertising.

**My reading (important for us):** (a) "Featured" and "Promoted" are not on the FTC's list of clearly understood terms; "Sponsored" or "Ad" is safer for a paid placement. (b) "Presented by [X]" is read by consumers as "funded but did not influence", which is accurate for a sponsor logo on a contest, but would be misleading on a paid ad dressed as content or if the sponsor influences results. (c) A logo alone is not enough.

### 3.3 .com Disclosures (March 2013)

Source: FTC PDF via https://search.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/press-releases/ftc-staff-revises-online-advertising-disclosure-guidelines/130312dotcomdisclosures.pdf (downloaded and read).

> Required disclosures must be clear and conspicuous. In evaluating whether a disclosure is likely to be clear and conspicuous, advertisers should consider its placement in the ad and its proximity to the relevant claim. The closer the disclosure is to the claim to which it relates, the better. Additional considerations include: the prominence of the disclosure; whether it is unavoidable; whether other parts of the ad distract attention from the disclosure ...

It also says to place disclosures "as close as possible to the triggering claim". It is staff guidance, not binding.

### 3.4 California

I found no California statute that specifically requires affiliate or sponsor disclosure. The general rules:

> It is unlawful for any person ... with intent directly or indirectly to dispose of real or personal property or to perform services ... to make or disseminate ... including over the Internet, any statement ... which is untrue or misleading, and which is known, or which by the exercise of reasonable care should be known, to be untrue or misleading ...
> (B&P 17500, excerpt; updated Jan. 1, 1999)

> ... unfair competition shall mean and include any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising ...
> (B&P 17200)

My reading: an FTC-style deceptive format (an unlabelled paid listing) could also be attacked under 17200/17500. Not researched further.

### 3.5 Installer listings (contractor advertising)

> It is a misdemeanor for any person to advertise for construction or work of improvement covered by this chapter unless that person holds a valid license under this chapter in the classification so advertised ... "Advertise" ... includes ... any electronic transmission, or ... any directory under a listing for construction or work of improvement ...
> (B&P 7027.1(a),(b), excerpts; accessed Nov. 2, 2026)

And B&P 7027.2 lets an unlicensed person advertise only if the aggregate contract price is under $1,000 and the ad says the person is not licensed. **My reading:** an installer directory that lists installers for paid work could be a "directory ... listing" for work covered by the Contractors State License Law. I do not know whether seasonal light installation is "work of improvement" or which classification applies. This is the attorney's question, not an answer.

## 4. Privacy

### 4.1 CCPA "business" threshold

Source: https://california.public.law/codes/ca_civ_code_section_1798.140 (updated Jan. 1, 2026; accessed Oct. 5, 2026).

> "Business" means: (1) A sole proprietorship, partnership, limited liability company, corporation, association, or other legal entity that is organized or operated for the profit or financial benefit of its shareholders or other owners, that collects consumers' personal information ... that does business in the State of California, and that satisfies one or more of the following thresholds: (A) As of January 1 of the calendar year, had annual gross revenues in excess of twenty-five million dollars ($25,000,000) in the preceding calendar year, as adjusted ... (B) Alone or in combination, annually buys, sells, or shares the personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households. (C) Derives 50 percent or more of its annual revenues from selling or sharing consumers' personal information.

> "Sell," "selling," "sale," or "sold," means ... making available, transferring, or otherwise communicating ... a consumer's personal information by the business to a third party for monetary or other valuable consideration. ... a business does not sell personal information when: (A) A consumer uses or directs the business to intentionally: (i) Disclose personal information. (ii) Interact with one or more third parties.

> "Consent" means any freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the consumer's wishes ... for a narrowly defined particular purpose. Acceptance of a general or broad terms of use ... does not constitute consent. ... agreement obtained through use of dark patterns does not constitute consent.

**My reading:**
- Thresholds (A) and (B) are far above our scale. The $25M figure is CPI-adjusted upward; I did not retrieve the current adjusted number.
- **(C) is the one to watch.** If installer lead fees (payment for contact details) ever became half of revenue, the "derives 50 percent or more of its annual revenues from selling or sharing" test could be met, even with tiny volume. Today Featured events and affiliates make it unlikely.
- Entrant-directed quote requests (the person clicks "Get a quote" and ticks a box naming the installer) look like the "consumer ... directs" exception, but the statute leaves open whether a *paid* lead product is a "sale". The attorney decides.
- Subsection (d)(4) lets any non-qualifying business "voluntarily certify" to the CPPA; we should not.
- Related: Civil Code 1798.83 ("Shine the Light") has a carve-out: "A business with fewer than 20 full-time or part-time employees is exempt" (updated Jan. 1, 2006; accessed Oct. 26, 2026).

### 4.2 CalOPPA

Source: https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_22575 (updated Jan. 1, 2014; accessed Oct. 5, 2026).

> An operator of a commercial Web site or online service that collects personally identifiable information through the Internet about individual consumers residing in California who use or visit its commercial Web site or online service shall conspicuously post its privacy policy on its Web site ... An operator shall be in violation of this subdivision only if the operator fails to post its policy within 30 days after being notified of noncompliance.

> The privacy policy ... shall ... Identify the categories of personally identifiable information that the operator collects ... and the categories of third-party persons or entities with whom the operator may share that personally identifiable information ... Describe the process by which the operator notifies consumers ... of material changes ... Identify its effective date. ... Disclose how the operator responds to Web browser "do not track" signals ... Disclose whether other parties may collect personally identifiable information about an individual consumer's online activities over time and across different Web sites ...
> (B&P 22575(a),(b), excerpts)

22576 makes it a violation to fail to comply with 22575 or with the posted policy "Knowingly and willfully" or "Negligently and materially". 22577(a) defines PII to include name, address, email address and phone number. **My reading:** it applies to us regardless of size once we collect emails or sign-ins. Our privacy page must list categories shared with third parties (installers, organizers), say how "do not track" is handled, state an effective date, and match actual practice (22576(b) makes breach of our own policy a violation).

## 5. Sales tax (CDTFA)

cdtfa.ca.gov/lawguides worked; the publication PDFs I tried returned 404. I found no regulation that directly addresses selling a paid placement or listing on our own site. What I read:

> The design, editing, or hosting of an electronic website in which no tangible personal property is transferred to the client is a service, the charge for which is not subject to tax.
> (Reg. 1540(b)(1)(E), Advertising Agencies and Commercial Artists; accessed 2026-11-04)

> The following and similar fees and commissions are not taxable when they are separately stated ... Media commissions or fees received for placement of advertising ...
> (Reg. 1540(b)(1)(F)(1))

> If the true object of the contract is the service per se, the transaction is not subject to tax ...
> (Reg. 1501, Service Enterprises Generally)

**My reading only:** California's sales tax centers on sales of tangible personal property, and these regulations treat advertising placement and website services as non-taxable services. They concern agencies, not a website selling its own ad space, and they pre-date some digital rules. I would not rely on this. Treat it as a CPA question. I did not retrieve Reg. 1502 beyond its coverage of computer programs, so software/digital-product treatment is unverified.

## 8. Changes this implies for our product and docs

(Suggestions only; no files were edited.)

- **`docs/contest-business-model.md`:** replace the 17534 line with the real provisions (Penal 319-322; B&P 17539 to 17539.35; AG page). Remove "I did not research FTC disclosure rules".
- **`docs/rules-review.md`:** state that 17539.15 is a sweepstakes-mailing statute and the "contest" test is 17539.3(e) (consideration). Update the section 17534 and 17539.15 notes.
- **`public/rules.js`:** (a) rename the organizer from "Sponsor" to "Organizer" so it does not clash with product sponsors; (b) state the prize's approximate retail value, since 17539.1(a)(6) requires "exact nature and approximate value" when a prize is offered (currently "Prizes have no cash value" and a free-text prize); (c) keep the free-entry line but consider replacing "PURCHASE WILL NOT IMPROVE YOUR CHANCES" with plain language tied to voting; (d) state the date winners are announced (17539.15(j) style); (e) add a line that sponsors pay for visibility only and do not judge or vote; (f) ask the attorney whether to limit eligibility by state.
- **"Presented by" and sponsor labels (`public/contest.html`, `public/organizer.html`):** per the FTC guide, "Presented by" signals funding without influence; use "Sponsored by" or "Ad" for paid placement, and add one line under the Sponsors box: "Sponsors pay for visibility. They do not judge or vote." Label Featured events "Sponsored" or "Ad" next to the listing, not only on a detail page.
- **Quote-lead consent text:** make the checkbox name the installer and the data shared (name, email, phone, note), say it is optional and separate from entering or voting, not pre-ticked, and say what the installer may do with it. Avoid bundling with terms of use (CCPA "consent" definition).
- **`public/privacy.html`:** line 26 ("We do not sell your information or share it with advertisers or data brokers") and line 52 (quote requests are sent to installers) should be reconciled; add an effective date, a do-not-track statement, categories shared with third parties, and a plain description of affiliate links (already present for Amazon; add ticket partners). Re-check after any paid lead product launches.
- **Affiliate links:** put a short disclosure next to each link or group ("We earn a commission if you buy"), not only in the privacy policy.
- **Installer directory:** hold paid installer listings until question 10 is answered; consider displaying a CSLB license number field.
- **Record-keeping:** keep contest rules, entries, vote logs and winner lists for at least two years after prizes (17539.2(d) pattern), even though it may not be required.
- **`docs/status.md`:** note that this brief exists and that no code or product text was changed.
